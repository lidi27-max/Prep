  • Speaking

    What If? World Cup Speaking Corners

    • B1+ to B2
    • 2nd, 3rd & mixed conditionals
    • Station rotation
    • 40–50 min

    Ten situation cards go up around the room and groups rotate between them. Each card has three questions in the same order: a hypothetical present, its consequences, then an imagined past. The questions force the target structure, so students practise the grammar while arguing about something they care about. The theme ties into Ecuador's 2026 World Cup campaign.

    CORNER: Ecuador reaches the final

    Ecuador reaches the World Cup Final for the first time in history.

    • What would happen if Ecuador reached the World Cup Final?
    • How would the country celebrate?
    • How would Ecuadorian football be different today if Ecuador had reached a final in the past?

    CORNER: Robots referee the World Cup

    Human referees are replaced by robots and artificial intelligence.

    • What would happen if robots refereed World Cup matches?
    • Would football be more impartial?
    • How would famous matches have been different if robots had been used in the past?

    Running Dictation: Chance and Choices

    • B1+
    • Vocabulary: luck, fate, decisions
    • Group, kinesthetic
    • 30 min

    Full model sentences go on the wall. A runner reads and memorises one, then dictates it to the writer, who fills in the gapped version. Each of the four groups gets the sentences in a different order, so teams cannot copy each other. A short story gap-fill then recycles every target item in a new context.

    Wall sentence

    “Her chance encounter with the CEO at the conference turned into a lucky break that opened many doors for her career.”

    Group 2 worksheet

    1. You'll in trouble if you misbehave again! You won't this time.
    2. Her with the CEO at the conference turned into a that opened many doors for her career.

    Follow-up story

    María had a with a guy on the street, and it turned into a for her. … After that, she made the to accept his proposal; she didn't want to in a boring job!

    Gerunds and Infinitives: Guided Discovery

    • B1+
    • Inductive grammar
    • Pairs, then whole class
    • 25 min

    Students work out the rule before I state it. Each section shows authentic-sounding examples, asks them to name the pattern, and then tests it straight away. The rule is something they notice, not something they copy, which makes it stick better.

    Look at the sentences

    “What a weird thing to do.”  ·  “The mall is a terrible place to go.”

    1. What part of speech are the bold words? nouns / adjectives
    2. What comes after the bold words? a gerund / an infinitive

    Complete the sentences

    1. Why do adults waste time going / to go to things like book signings?
    2. OK, this is worth waiting / to wait for!

    Fixed expressions, like have fun, are usually followed by a gerund / an infinitive.

  • Reading & writing

    Reading Practice: The Future of Remote Work

    • B2
    • Inference & author's purpose
    • Exam-style multiple choice
    • Practice hour

    These are comprehension questions I wrote for an argumentative text. They go beyond finding facts: students locate information by section, explain why the author uses a statistic, and infer the author's stance. These are the skills the end-of-level reading exam and essay writing both depend on.

    1. The author mentions “250 hours annually” in the 2nd paragraph to emphasize:
      a) how little time people spend commuting   b) the exact amount employers should pay for travel   c) the significant amount of time saved by remote work   d) the maximum legal commuting time
    2. What can be inferred about the author's opinion on hybrid work models?
      a) They are the perfect solution for all companies.   b) They represent a reasonable compromise.   c) They are too complicated to implement.   d) They will eventually fail.
  • Reading & writing

    Thesis Statement Review

    • B2
    • Persuasive essay
    • Evaluate, then fix
    • 15 min

    This is one step in my persuasive essay sequence: thesis, introduction, body paragraphs, conclusion, each with its own slides and a draft template. Students judge real-looking thesis statements against a clear standard (a claim plus three reasons). The weak examples show the most common problems, such as announcing the topic, describing instead of arguing, or giving only one reason.

    Read each thesis statement. If it is strong, write “Good” and underline the three reasons. If it is weak, write “Weak” and explain the problem.

    1. Introducing mindfulness programs in the workplace is important to reduce stress, improve focus, and increase job satisfaction.
    2. This essay will talk about the benefits of learning a second language.
    3. Social media includes safety protocols, different settings, and a variety of platforms to choose from.
    4. Banning sugary drinks in schools is a good idea because it helps students stay healthy.

    Key: 1 Good · 2 Weak, announces the topic with no claim · 3 Weak, describes instead of arguing · 4 Weak, only one reason

    Find the Mistake: Error Hunt

    • B1 to B2
    • Accuracy & L1 interference
    • Team game with slides
    • 20 min

    Each set has three sentences and exactly one error. The errors target typical mistakes of Spanish-speaking learners (I'm agree, according with, she can to speak). Teams race to find the mistake and correct it, and the slides reveal the answers.

    B. He should visit his grandmother more often.

    Persuasive Essay Exam and Analytic Rubric

    • B2
    • Persuasive essay
    • 5 criteria × 5 bands
    • 10 points

    This is the writing exam I use with English V students. The prompt takes a real local controversy and asks students to argue a position. The analytic rubric scores five criteria from 2 to 0.4 points each, so students see exactly where their points went, and different teachers mark consistently. The criteria match what the essay sequence above teaches: thesis, topic sentences, cohesion and persuasive register.

    A dog recently became the center of a court case in Ecuador, where a judge had to decide who should take care of him. Some people believe animals deserve legal rights and protection, just like humans. Others think animals should be cared for, but giving them legal rights creates unnecessary problems. Which opinion do you agree with? Explain your position using specific reasons and examples.

    Unit Quiz: Life Choices and Conditionals

    • B1+ to B2
    • Vocabulary · grammar · reading
    • Weighted scoring
    • 20 min

    This is a short unit quiz with three sections and points for each item. Vocabulary is tested in context, never as isolated definitions. Grammar items mix present, past and mixed time frames, so students have to choose the right conditional instead of repeating one pattern. A True / False / Not Given reading section completes it.

    Part 1 · Vocabulary in context (0.5 each)

    a) chance encounter · b) deliberate decision · c) fate · d) fortunate · e) life-changing experience · f) lucky break · g) path · h) wind up

    1. If you don't take better care of yourself, you'll being really sick.
    2. I made a never to go back to that store. Their salespeople are so rude!

    Part 2 · Conditionals (0.5 each)

    1. If we (not take) a wrong turn, we wouldn't be lost now.
    2. He wouldn't be so tired if he (go) to bed earlier last night.
    3. If you (win) the lottery next week, what would you do?

    Feedback Bank: 5-Minute Video Pitch

    • B2
    • Oral presentation
    • Written feedback
    • Reusable comment bank

    These are comments I wrote while grading students' recorded pitches. They are grouped so feedback is specific, balanced and fast to give across large classes. Each one names something the student can change next time, such as words to re-pronounce, the exact grammar slip, or timing, instead of a general “good job”.

    Strengths

    • You clearly mapped out the presentation, transitioning smoothly from the causes to the consequences.
    • Your solutions were very concrete and actionable.
    • Finishing with a question was engaging.

    Language

    • Check the grammar: you said “many families spends…”, “this issues”.
    • Watch the ends of words; sometimes you don't pronounce the full word.
    • Check word stress, for example imPORtance.

    Delivery

    • Pace yourself and articulate each word clearly.
    • Don't read from the slides.
    • Your partner spoke more than you. Share the time equally.
